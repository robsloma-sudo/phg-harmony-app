# Menu design theory and menu engineering: evidence base

Read on 2026-09-28 by a research sub-agent of the PHG Menu Designer. Sources were read through Firecrawl scrapes of
the PDF or HTML (WebFetch was blocked by the proxy). Each source says what was actually seen: full text, extraction
from full text, or abstract only.

## Sources

**[1] Yang, S. S. (2012). "Eye movements on restaurant menus: A revisitation on gaze motion and consumer scanpaths."** *Int. J. Hospitality Management* 31(3), 1021–1029.
Full text: https://psu.pb.unizin.org/app/uploads/sites/126/2018/12/Eye-Movement-2012-article.pdf
- Lab eye-tracking, n=25, two-page 8.5×11" menu, 12 pt sans on cream.
- **Diners read menus like a book.** Scanpaths were closer to Gallup's (1987) sequential path than to the textbook "sweet spot" or "golden triangle": standardised Levenshtein distance 0.377 vs 0.523 (t₂₄ = −6.62, p < .0001).
- 52% followed exactly top-left → down the left page → top of the right page for their first five fixations.
- **No frequency sweet spot**: fixations did not differ across the four main areas (F = 0.97, p = .413). There was no consistent last-look spot either. The chef/restaurant info block got 9 fixations across all 25 subjects.
- **Diners read rather than scan**: 239 s mean viewing time, about 155 wpm. Entrées are chosen first and read most slowly (42.6 s dwell).
- Doerfler's 1978 right-page map and the textbook scanpath were "never empirically validated".
- *Quality:* lab study, one menu, no sales data. This is the key study contesting the golden triangle.

**[2] Smith, Guliuzo, Benedict & Chaparro (2019). "An Eye-Tracking Analysis of a Restaurant Menu."** *Proc. HFES* 63(1).
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337420373 (extraction from full text)
- n=29 students, one one-page menu in colour and in black and white.
- In black and white, the first gaze went top-left and reading followed book order. In colour, the first gaze went to the **bright orange centre section**. Salience beats any fixed "spot".
- Heat maps: the middle and upper-left got the most attention. Participants said they wanted photos.

**[3] Yang, Kimes & Sessarego (2009). "$ or Dollars: Effects of Menu-price Formats on Restaurant Checks."** *Cornell Hospitality Report* 9(8).
https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/d9504484-4912-4291-a65c-f5b44461302b/download (full text)
- Field experiment at the CIA's St. Andrew's Café, n=201 tables, three formats: "$20.00", "20." and "twenty dollars".
- **Numeral-only prices: parties spent $3.70 more than average; "$" or written-out prices: $1.85 less.** That is a $5.55 per table gap, about 8% per person.
- "$" and "dollars" did equally badly: any mention of currency hurts.
- Prices ending in 9 signal value; prices ending in 0 signal quality (Naipaul & Parsa 2001).
- *Quality:* one venue, lunch only, not widely replicated.

**[4] Yang & Lynn (2009). "Wine List Characteristics Associated with Greater Wine Sales."** Cornell Hospitality Report.
https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstreams/dec6602d-1877-469a-a51e-503ca730b053/download
- 270 US wine lists against 12 months of distributor sales data (correlational).
- Higher sales went with:
  - the wine list **printed on the food menu**;
  - **no "$"** (marginal, p < .08);
  - named well-regarded wineries;
  - a **"Reserve" section** (p < .01).
- Lower sales went with **organising by "wine style"** (p < .01).
- Casual dining: sales peaked at about **150 bottles**, and more low-cost wines helped.
- Not related to sales: by-the-glass, flights, price position (tucked in or right-justified), type size, tasting notes, vintage, bin numbers.

**[5] Corsi, Mueller & Lockshin (2010). "How does item order and other information impact wine menu choice?"** 5th AWBR Conf.
- Discrete choice experiment, n=1,258.
- **Sorting by ascending price → cheaper choices.**
- Sorting by sensory progression (light → full) raised willingness to pay by $0.18 per glass. Price-sorted lists felt easier (37 s faster).
- With non-price order, diners used long descriptions, food matches and awards about 2% more.

**[6] Dayan & Bar-Hillel (2011). "Nudge to nobesity II: Menu positions influence food orders."** *Judgment and Decision Making* 6(4).
https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/journal/11/11407/jdm11407.html (full text)
- Lab study (n=240) plus a real café field study (about 950 orders).
- **Items first or last in their category were chosen about 20% more often than the same items mid-list** (55% vs 45%).
- No primacy vs recency difference. Second and penultimate positions benefit too.
- Held for categories of 4 to 10 items.
- Advice: "Put the food you want to encourage at the extremes of the menu listings."
- *Quality:* consistent lab plus field evidence. This is the one layout rule with field data behind it.

**[7] Kincaid & Corsun (2003). "Are consultants blowing smoke? An empirical test of the impact of menu layout on item sales."** *IJCHM* 15(4). **Abstract only.**
- Swapping the contents of pages 2 and 3 of a three-page menu for 4 months produced **no significant sales change**.
- It does *not* show that boxes work, despite how it is sometimes cited.
- Bowen & Morris (1995), "Menu design: can menus sell?": abstract only, gives no numbers.

**[8] Wansink, Painter & van Ittersum (2001). "Descriptive Menu Labels' Effect on Sales."** *CHRAQ* 42(6).
- In a cafeteria field test, descriptive labels raised sales by **27%** and raised ratings of quality, value and the restaurant.
- Price tolerance barely moved ($3.08 to $3.30).
- Four label types: geographic, nostalgic, sensory, brand. Use them sparingly and truthfully, because unmerited labels backfire.
- *Caveat:* Wansink resigned in 2018 after a misconduct finding. This paper is not retracted, but treat 27% as indicative only.

**[9] Horváth et al. (2022). "Examining a menu on the basis of the Kasavana–Smith model in a Hungarian restaurant."** *Deturope* 14(1).
https://www.deturope.eu/pdfs/det/2022/01/06.pdf
- **Kasavana & Smith (1982) matrix**:
  - Popularity threshold = 70% × (100% ÷ number of items in the category).
  - Profitability = contribution margin against the category's average margin.
- **Stars**: keep, and staff recommend them.
- **Puzzles**: give them prominence and recommendation.
- **Plowhorses**: raise the price a little or trim the portion.
- **Dogs**: replace.
- Case: the menu shrank from 49–63 items to 21. Margin went from about 120% to about 180%, **but revenue stagnated**.
- Limitations: the method uses food cost only (ignores labour), and relative thresholds always fill every quadrant.

**[10] Schwartz, B. (2006). "Menu Engineering: A Scientific Approach to Improved Menu Profitability."** *Indian Gaming*.
- Optimise money margin per item, not food-cost percentage.
- **Puzzles: move them somewhere more visible and rename them to be more appealing.**
- *Quality:* practitioner article, no data.

**[11] Johns, Edwards & Hartwell (2013). "Menu Choice: Satisfaction or Overload?"** *J. Culinary Sci. & Tech.* 11(3). **Abstract only.**
- The ideal is about **6 choices per category** in quick service.
- In fine dining it is **7–8 starters and desserts and about 10 mains**.

**[12] Bujisic et al. (2024/2026). "Anchoring decisions: the role of decoy pricing in consumer choices."** *Int. Hospitality Review* 40(1).
- Scenario experiments: a decoy raised choice of the target bundle from 65% to 95%, from 44.7% to 71.4%, and from 82% to 93%.
- It works when the decoy is priced the same as the target but clearly worse. **This conflicts with [13].**

**[13] Yang & Lynn (2014). "More Evidence Challenging the Robustness and Usefulness of the Attraction Effect."** *J. Marketing Research* 51(4). **Abstract only.**
- 91 attempts produced only 11 reliable decoy effects.
- **When options have verbal descriptions or pictures, as on menus, the effect falls to chance.**

**[14] Yim & Yoo (2020). "Are Digital Menus Really Better than Traditional Menus?"** *J. Interactive Marketing* 50. **Abstract only.**
- Tablet menus led to more enjoyment and to diners ordering more and faster, **but only for unfamiliar foods**.
- The benefit fades for familiar foods.

**[15] Elshaer et al. (2025). "Scrolling the Menu, Posting the Meal."** *Tourism and Hospitality* 6(5).
- A digital menu's visual appeal and informativeness predict desire for the food and ease of choosing.
- The effects are stronger when menu visuals match the real dish.
- *Quality:* weak, self-report survey.

**[16] Sensory Trust. "Designing with clear print and large print"** (follows RNIB and UKAAF).
https://www.sensorytrust.org.uk/resources/guidance/designing-with-clear-and-large-print
- Clear print: **minimum 12 pt, ideally 14 pt**. Large print: 16 pt or more.
- Use a clear sans or a sturdy serif in regular or semi-bold. No script, light weights, underlining or justified text. Use bold, capitals and italics sparingly.
- Keep reversed (light-on-dark) text to headings and short blocks. **Never use colour as the only signal of meaning.**

**[17] UK Food Standards Agency.** (a) *Allergen Information for Non-Prepacked Foods*, Ipsos, 2025 (n=964); (b) FSA allergen guidance, 2025–26.
- The 14 UK allergens must be identified. Written information is best practice, with a sign telling guests how to ask.
- 71% of allergic diners rely on menus; **77% like allergen symbols on menus**.
- Trust: printed 87%, digital 80%, verbal 75%.

**[18] UK Trading Standards: licensed premises** (Business Companion; Home Office). England and Wales law.
- Spirits come in 25 or 35 ml measures. Wine by the glass comes in 125 or 175 ml, and **the size must be shown on the menu**. Draught comes in ⅓, ½ or ⅔ pint.
- Small measures must be available and shown. Prices must include VAT. Show ABV "where appropriate".

*Context only (search snippets, not evidence):* a Technomic/PYMNTS survey found that 88% of diners prefer paper menus to QR codes. Trade blogs say body text should be 12–14 pt.

## Contradictions (resolved for PHG)

1. **Golden triangle.** It is unsupported ([1][2][7]). Diners read in book order, and salience draws the first glance.
2. **List position.** Edges beat the middle by about 20% ([6]). There is no primacy vs recency difference.
3. **Dollar signs.** Direction is consistent, drop them ([3][4]), but the evidence is thin.
4. **Decoys.** They work for comparable bundles and sizes ([12]) but vanish among described items ([13]).
5. **Descriptions.** Wansink's +27% carries a credibility caveat. Wine tasting notes showed no sales link ([4]). Keep copy honest and sparing.
6. **Wine order.** A "style" layout was correlated with lower sales ([4]), but sensory order raised willingness to pay in an experiment ([5]). **Never sort by ascending price.**
7. **Right-aligned price column.** [4] found no sales effect of price position, and trade advice often says to bury prices. **Rob's scorecard (criterion 3) requires one aligned price column or one consistent inline style. For PHG the scorecard wins.**
8. **Digital vs paper.** Digital helps with unfamiliar items ([14]). Allergen trust is higher on print ([17]). Keep a paper option.

## Rules for a menu designer (evidence-graded)

1. Put the items to sell **first or last** in their category. Second or penultimate also works. [6] **High**
2. Don't design around a golden triangle; assume book-order reading. [1][2][7] **High**
3. Use salience (colour, weight, accent) sparingly, one or two per page, on Stars or Puzzles. [2][9][10] **Medium**
4. Give the main section (cocktails at a bar, entrées at a restaurant) the most room and the clearest hierarchy. [1] **Medium**
5. Prices are bare numerals with no "$", no currency word and no ".00". [3][4] **Medium**
6. Never order a section by ascending price. [5] **Medium**
7. Classify with Kasavana–Smith before laying out, using the venue's cost and sales data (`phg.menu_engineering_snapshots`, `phg.menu_item_evaluations`). Feature Stars, reposition and rename Puzzles, don't feature Plowhorses, and flag Dogs. [9][10] **High**
8. When pruning, watch total revenue as well as margin. [9] **Medium**
9. Aim for about 6 items per category in quick service and 7–10 in fine dining. Split larger lists into subsections. [11] **Medium**
10. Write descriptive copy (geographic, nostalgic, sensory, brand) only from facts, and use it sparingly. [8][5] **Medium**
11. Use decoys only between comparable options (glass sizes, bundles), never among described items. [12][13] **Medium**
12. A "Reserve" or "Cellar" anchor section at the top or end of the wine list is associated with higher sales. [4] **Medium**
13. Put wines by the glass on the main menu. [4] **Medium**
14. Body text at least 12 pt, ideally 14 pt, with a large-print version at 16 pt or more. [16] **High**
15. Use regular or semi-bold faces: no script, light weights, all-caps paragraphs or justified text. [16] **High**
16. Put light-on-dark text only in headings and short blocks. Use dark on light for long lists in dim rooms. [16] **High**
17. Never let colour alone carry meaning. [16][17] **High**
18. Use allergen symbols plus a key, and a line on how to ask. Keep a printed allergen matrix alongside QR menus. [17] **High (UK)**
19. On digital menus apply the same edge rule, and use honest photos only. [6][14][15] **Medium**
20. Re-run menu engineering after each redesign. A/B test position swaps by day. [6][7][9] **High**
21. Keep story or chef blocks out of prime space. [1] **Medium**
